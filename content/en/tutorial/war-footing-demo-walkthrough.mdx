Every turn represents one week. When the invasion begins, the army that meets the enemy is the army you built beforehand.

## The Weekly Turn Loop, Step by Step

Every turn is a week, and every week should follow roughly the same sequence. Skipping steps is how players end up with a beautifully equipped army that has nowhere to stand and no money behind it.

| Phase | What to do | Mistake to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Read reports | Check intelligence, readiness, and stability before spending anything | Clicking through reports and reacting blind |
| 2. Set the budget | Allocate spending and protect a reserve | Committing the entire weekly budget on turn one |
| 3. Procurement | Order equipment from foreign suppliers or domestic industry | Ordering kit that won't arrive before the invasion |
| 4. Train and assign | Build units and raise their readiness | Leaving units understrength because you overbought |
| 5. Politics and diplomacy | Address stability and diplomatic obligations | Treating politics as optional busywork |
| 6. Deployment | Place units on the sectors you expect to be attacked | Spreading thin across every sector equally |
| 7. End turn | Confirm orders and advance the week | Ending the turn without checking what's arriving next week |

For the first few turns, resist the urge to buy anything expensive. Spend that time reading the scenario brief, identifying the likely axis of attack, and mapping your budget runway — the number of weeks your current spending can be sustained. Players who front-load purchases usually discover around mid-game that they can no longer pay for training, and untrained units with good rifles lose to trained units with old ones.

A simple rule: never let your reserve drop below the cost of one full week of training and maintenance. That reserve is what lets you respond when a scenario throws a political crisis or an early border incident at you.

## Budget, Procurement, and Equipment Trade-Offs

Procurement is where most demo runs are won or lost. The game offers over 180 equipment types across small arms, armor, artillery, aircraft, and air defense, and each one carries a distinct trade-off between cost, capability, and availability. There is no universally correct shopping list — only a list that fits your scenario and your doctrine.

| Category | Primary role | Trade-off to weigh |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small arms | Basic infantry lethality | Cheap and fast to acquire, but low individual impact |
| Armor | Mobile protected firepower | Expensive and slow to deliver, decisive once ready |
| Artillery | Indirect fire and attrition | Powerful, but demands logistics and support |
| Aircraft | Reach, strike, and reconnaissance | Highest cost, tightest availability |
| Air defense | Protects everything else you bought | Passive value, so it's easy to under-buy |

Two sourcing routes compete for your attention. Foreign suppliers typically offer higher-capability equipment, but delivery timing and diplomatic conditions can bite you. Domestic industry is slower and often less advanced, but it keeps money and jobs inside the country — which quietly supports the political stability that funds your next budget.

| Sourcing route | Strength | Weakness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Foreign suppliers | Better capability, sometimes faster | Conditional, politically exposed, delivery risk |
| Domestic industry | Political goodwill, resilience, predictable | Slower fielding, older designs |

A balanced procurement plan usually mixes both. Buying exclusively abroad makes you dependent on partners who may not share your urgency; buying exclusively at home leaves you outgunned in the opening weeks.

## Politics and Stability: The Resource You Can't Buy Back

Military spending never happens in a vacuum, and this War Footing demo walkthrough treats stability as a first-class resource for that reason. Political stability is what keeps the budget flowing. Diplomatic obligations compete for the same money and attention. Ignore either and the consequences arrive at the worst possible moment.

| Lever | Short-term gain | Long-term cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heavy military spending | Faster force building | Stability drain and future budget pressure |
| Concessions to foreign partners | Access to better equipment | Obligations that limit your freedom to act |
| Domestic industry contracts | Local political goodwill | Slower deliveries and older designs |
| Ignoring political warnings | Saved time and money now | Budget cuts later, exactly when you need funding |

The practical habit is to check stability every single week, not just when it flashes red. A small, steady investment in political health is cheaper than an emergency correction. Community reports from the demo period suggest that most failed runs had a healthy army and a collapsing home front — a mismatch that no amount of armor can fix.

## From Doctrine to Deployment: Winning the Operational Fight

The final act of any War Footing demo walkthrough is the fight itself. Battles resolve at the operational level — sector by sector, week by week. Outcomes depend on what you built: unit readiness, doctrine fit, equipment quality, and where you chose to deploy. You don't micromanage firefights. You live with your earlier decisions.

| Echelon | Best used for | Cost consideration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Company | Flexible coverage of secondary sectors | Cheap, but easy to overwhelm |
| Battalion | Holding a primary axis | Balanced cost and staying power |
| Brigade | Decisive sector or counterattack | Expensive; needs readiness and support to justify |

Combined arms matters. Armor without infantry is vulnerable in close terrain, infantry without artillery struggles to break prepared positions, and any formation without air defense becomes a target. Before you commit, confirm three things: are the units actually trained, does their doctrine match the terrain and mission, and is the equipment appropriate for the enemy you expect?

A short list of mistakes that show up again and again:

- Spending the whole budget on turn one and running dry mid-game
- Building units you can't equip, leaving them understrength
- Deploying evenly across every sector instead of weighting the likely attack
- Under-buying air defense because it never shows a dramatic result
- Treating political stability as a distraction rather than a funding source
