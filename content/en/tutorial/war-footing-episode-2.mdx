Speed decides wars — and speed is built long before the first shot is fired. That is the central lesson of **war footing episode 2**, the follow-up to our first look at how nations convert peacetime economies into wartime production engines. If a country cannot replace ships, missiles, and spare parts faster than it expends them, deterrence erodes and strategy becomes wishful thinking.

This installment of war footing episode 2 goes deeper into the mechanics: what a war footing actually is, which levers move an economy fastest, why procurement timelines balloon, and what a realistic checklist looks like for a nation that wants to be ready before a crisis forces its hand.

## What "War Footing" Actually Means

A war footing is the posture a nation adopts when defense production, procurement, and logistics are organized around winning a conflict rather than around routine peacetime budgeting. It is not simply spending more money. It is spending faster, deciding faster, and accepting a different set of trade-offs.

The historian Paul Kennedy's argument in *The Rise and Fall of the Great Powers* still frames the debate: in a long, drawn-out great-power war, victory tends to go to the side with the more productive base. Productivity — not just inventory — is the durable advantage.

Analysts describe the current situation bluntly. A defense industrial base that spent three decades optimizing for efficiency and regulatory compliance is structurally slow, while competitors who never fully demobilized their capacity can produce at scale. That mismatch is why war footing episode 2 focuses less on budget totals and more on throughput: how many hulls, airframes, and munitions actually leave the factory per year.

| Dimension | Peacetime Footing | War Footing |
|---|---|---|
| Decision cycle | Years of studies and requirements reviews | Weeks; empowered senior leaders |
| Contracting | Full federal acquisition rulebook | Streamlined, milestone-based awards |
| Supplier base | Consolidated prime contractors | Diversified primes plus small and mid-tier firms |
| Production capacity | Sized to steady-state demand | Surge capacity and dual-use plants |
| Allied role | Case-by-case | Deliberate burden sharing |
| Success metric | Cost avoidance and compliance | Delivered capability, on time |

The distinction matters because peacetime habits are sticky. Regulations written to prevent fraud and cost overruns quietly add years to every program, and those years are the difference between deterrence and a scramble.

## The Four Levers That Move an Economy Onto a War Footing

Analysts generally point to four levers that separate a peacetime industrial base from one that can surge. Pulling only one or two rarely works; they reinforce each other.

| Lever | What It Looks Like | Common Failure Mode |
|---|---|---|
| Urgency | Senior leadership sets delivery dates, not study milestones | Urgency fades once the headline news cycle moves on |
| Regulatory relief | Targeted waivers, simplified contracting, faster certification | Blanket deregulation that invites waste and fraud |
| Capacity expansion | New tooling, land, workforce pipelines, stockpiles | Factories built but no skilled labor to run them |
| Allied integration | Co-production and licensed manufacturing with partners | Political resistance to any foreign content |

**Urgency** is the master lever. In a genuine crisis, decision loops compress because the cost of delay is measured in lives. Ukraine's wartime production of drones and long-range systems is a live example of what happens when red tape is cut under existential pressure. Israel's layered air-defense production tells a similar story.

**Regulatory relief** is where most reform efforts stall. The goal is not to remove oversight but to shift the default from "prove you should be allowed" to "deliver, and we will audit."

**Capacity** takes the longest to build, which is precisely why it cannot wait for a war. New shipyards, machine tools, and munitions lines require years of lead time, permits, and trained workers.

**Allied integration** is the fastest lever to pull when domestic capacity is short — and the most politically difficult, because it touches domestic jobs and sovereignty concerns.

## Procurement: Why Timelines Balloon and How to Fix Them

Industry practitioners who deal with the Pentagon describe a system that is cumbersome by design. Bidding requires specialized software, and simply confirming who is an approved supplier for a given part can consume thousands of man-hours. Getting an alternative source approved can take years and often depends on winning over a single engineer.

Defense analysts frequently warn that major programs routinely take a decade or more to deliver less capability than originally promised, at two to three times the planned cost. The regulations behind that delay were written for good reasons — preventing fraud, waste, and abuse — but they compound into a structural tax on speed.

| Bottleneck | Why It Slows Delivery | Practical Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Permitting and land acquisition | Companies wait years before breaking ground | Pre-cleared industrial sites and fast-track permits |
| Contracting rules | Hundreds of pages of requirements per award | Milestone-based awards with post-audit |
| Single-source parts | One vendor controls spares and pricing | License third-party alternatives, as done on older fighters |
| Certification backlog | Alternate parts wait on individual approvals | Tier parts by criticality; streamline non-safety-critical items |
| Workforce shortages | No welders, machinists, or shipwrights | Fund trade pipelines and apprenticeships early |

The licensing question is instructive. On legacy fighters such as the F-15, F-16, and F-18, third parties were allowed to develop alternative parts, and competition improved cost and availability. On the newest stealth fighter, spares remain tightly controlled by the original manufacturer — even though the aircraft was largely funded by taxpayers and its operational readiness has hovered well below target. Opening that market to qualified suppliers is one of the cheapest readiness gains available.

A useful mental model from the defense literature: a war footing needs more "ratcatchers" — people obsessed with winning — and fewer pure regulators whose instinct is to protect process. War footing episode 2 keeps returning to that idea because process discipline without delivery discipline is just slow failure with good paperwork.

## Shipbuilding and Alliances: Where the Gap Is Widest

Nowhere is the peacetime-versus-wartime gap wider than in shipbuilding. Analysts estimate that China controls roughly half of global commercial shipbuilding capacity, with South Korea and Japan ranking second and third. The United States accounts for less than one percent.

That imbalance has consequences. A navy cannot grow faster than its industrial base allows, and building back domestic shipyards from a near-standing start would take far longer than any plausible conflict timeline. The pragmatic answer is layered:

- **Keep submarines domestic.** Undersea platforms carry the most sensitive technology and provide the most durable advantage.
- **Buy unmanned underwater systems broadly.** Many allied and commercial producers build capable systems, and undersea surveillance is where peer competitors are weakest.
- **Leverage allies for surface hulls.** Frigates, logistics vessels, and lower-end surface ships are candidates for co-production with partners who already have the yards and the labor.
- **Protect the intellectual property, not the rivets.** Heavy metal fabrication can happen abroad while sensitive electronics and combat systems are fitted at home.

Submarines matter disproportionately because peer competitors struggle with anti-submarine warfare. Detecting and tracking quiet submarines remains one of the hardest problems in naval warfare, and that asymmetry shapes everything from deterrence posture to war planning.

## A Practical Checklist for Getting on a War Footing

If you want a concrete way to evaluate whether a country, agency, or program is genuinely on a war footing, run it through this checklist. Each item is observable, and each one exposes the difference between rhetoric and readiness.

| Check | Green Flag | Red Flag |
|---|---|---|
| Decision authority | A single empowered leader can cut through reviews | Decisions require multi-agency consensus |
| Delivery dates | Public, dated commitments per program | Rolling schedules with no accountability |
| Stockpiles | Depth measured against war-game consumption rates | Depth measured against annual peacetime training |
| Supplier diversity | Multiple qualified sources per critical part | Sole-source dependency on one prime |
| Surge capacity | Idle but maintained lines ready to restart | Capacity permanently retired |
| Allied pipeline | Signed co-production agreements | Loosely worded letters of intent |
| Talent pipeline | Funded apprenticeships and trade schools | Reliance on contractors to train their own |

War-game findings reinforce the urgency. Repeated tabletop exercises involving a Taiwan contingency have found that long-range precision munitions can be exhausted in under a week of high-intensity fighting. If stockpiles run dry that fast, the deterrent value of those weapons depends entirely on how quickly they can be replenished — which is an industrial question, not a tactical one.

There is also a financing shift worth noting. Venture capital flowing into defense technology grew from roughly two billion dollars a year in the early 2010s to more than forty billion by the early 2020s. Private capital now funds many of the firms pushing autonomy, space-based communications, and AI-enabled systems. For a deeper look at the research driving these assessments, the [Center for Strategic and International Studies](https://www.csis.org/) publishes ongoing work on defense industrial base capacity and procurement reform.

## FAQ

**What does it mean to be on a war footing?**

Being on a war footing means organizing defense production, procurement, and logistics around delivering capability fast rather than around routine peacetime budgeting. It prioritizes speed, throughput, and surge capacity over cost avoidance and procedural compliance. War footing episode 2 stresses that this is a posture, not a single budget line.

**Why can't a country simply spend more money to get there faster?**

Money does not compress physics or training. Shipyards, machine tools, and skilled welders take years to develop, and regulatory approvals add years on top. Analysts consistently find that major programs run a decade or longer and cost two to three times original estimates, so additional funding alone does not fix the timeline problem.

**Which parts of a defense industrial base should stay domestic?**

The most sensitive technologies — submarines, stealth aircraft, advanced electronics, and nuclear systems — should remain domestic or tightly controlled. Lower-end surface ships, logistics vessels, and many unmanned systems are reasonable candidates for allied co-production, provided intellectual property and combat systems stay protected.

**How would a nation know it is genuinely ready?**

Run readiness against war-game consumption rates rather than peacetime training rates. If stockpiles, supplier diversity, and surge capacity can absorb the first weeks of a high-intensity conflict and still replenish, the posture is real. If not, the war footing is still theoretical — and war footing episode 2 exists precisely to close that gap.
