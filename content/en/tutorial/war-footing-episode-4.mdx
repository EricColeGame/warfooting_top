War Footing Episode 4 is where your spreadsheet finally has to fight. If you have followed this tutorial series from the start, you already know how to read a budget line, place a procurement order, and keep your political backers from walking away. Now comes the hard part: making sure the army you assembled is the army that actually shows up. War Footing Episode 4 covers the late-game stretch, when every remaining week is a chance to close a capability gap — or to widen one you will never be able to fix once the invasion begins.

That matters because you never take direct control of a battle here. You are the Minister of Defense in a turn-based strategy game from Strategist's Den Games, and combat resolves at the operational level, sector by sector and week by week. The outcome depends almost entirely on decisions you made long before the first shot was fired. Get the late game right, and your preparation does the fighting for you.

## The Four Phases of a War Footing Campaign

Every turn in the game represents a single week, which sounds generous until you realize how much each week is expected to accomplish. You source equipment from foreign suppliers or your own domestic industry, train and assign units, and manage political stability so the budget keeps flowing. Breaking a campaign into four rough phases makes it far easier to decide what any given week is actually for.

| Phase | What you are really doing | Where the money goes | Cost of neglecting it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early | Setting doctrine and locking in a force structure | Small, flexible orders | You buy equipment your army cannot support |
| Mid | Bulk procurement and raising formations | The largest share of the budget | Combined-arms gaps appear at first contact |
| Late | Readiness, training, and assignment | Training and sustainment | Fully equipped units arrive unready to fight |
| Contact | Deployment and sector battles | Almost nothing new | The wrong units sit in the wrong sectors |

The most common late-game failure is treating the final weeks like the early ones. Early on, flexibility is an asset. Late in the campaign, flexibility becomes a liability, because a formation still waiting on equipment when the invasion starts is a formation that will not fight.

## Procurement Priorities: What to Buy Before the Clock Runs Out

### The 180-Equipment Problem

The game ships with more than 180 equipment types spread across small arms, armor, artillery, aircraft, and air defense systems. Each one carries its own trade-offs in cost, capability, and availability. That means the real skill is not finding good equipment — it is refusing to buy most of it.

| Category | Primary job | Where it hurts | Late-game guidance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small arms | Equipping and sustaining infantry | Cheap but dates quickly | Top up existing units; avoid over-investing |
| Armor | Breaking through and blunting breakthroughs | Expensive and support-hungry | Buy only alongside supporting arms |
| Artillery | Shaping battles before contact | Slower to field than it looks | Often the best late-game return per dollar |
| Aircraft | Reach, strike, and reconnaissance | Costly with tight availability | Commit early or skip entirely |
| Air defense | Protecting everything above the ground fight | Easy to postpone, painful to lack | Never leave this category empty |

### Domestic Industry vs. Foreign Suppliers

You can source equipment from foreign suppliers or from your own domestic industry, and the choice is not purely economic. Foreign suppliers can plug a capability gap quickly, but they come with diplomatic strings and availability limits. Domestic production is slower, but it tends to be more predictable across a long campaign and generally plays better with the political establishment that signs off on your budget.

| Sourcing route | Strength | Weakness | Best suited for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Foreign supplier | Fast access to capable kit | Diplomatic obligations, limited availability | Plugging an urgent capability gap |
| Domestic industry | Predictable sustainment, political goodwill | Slower delivery timelines | Long campaigns with stable budgets |

### Buy in Packets, Not Pieces

A useful rule for War Footing Episode 4: raise and outfit units from company to brigade level as complete packages rather than accumulating orphan equipment. A brigade with coherent equipment, a clear role, and enough training weeks behind it will outperform a larger collection of mismatched pieces almost every time.

## Politics Is a Resource, Not a Cutscene

Military spending does not happen in a vacuum. Diplomatic obligations and domestic political stability compete for exactly the same resources your procurement office wants, and ignoring either one has consequences that surface as budget interruptions at the worst possible moment. By the time you reach War Footing Episode 4, your political margin is usually thinner than your equipment margin.

| Demand | Who is asking | What it consumes | What happens if you refuse |
|---|---|---|---|
| Procurement contracts | Your own force | Money and political capital | Readiness gaps you cannot close in time |
| Domestic stability | Civilian leadership | A share of the budget | Budget interruptions mid-campaign |
| Diplomatic obligations | Foreign partners | Procurement freedom | Reduced supplier access and goodwill |

A practical habit: at the start of every turn, check political stability before you check your shopping list. If stability is trending down, the budget you plan to spend next week may not exist.

## Readiness, Doctrine, and Deployment: Winning Battles You Never See

Battles resolve at the operational level, and the game rewards combined arms and knowing your enemy. Four factors do most of the work, and all four are things you control before contact.

| Factor | What it means in practice | How to improve it before contact |
|---|---|---|
| Unit readiness | Whether a formation can fight on day one | Protect training weeks; stop reassigning units late |
| Doctrine fit | Whether the unit's design matches your plan | Buy equipment that supports one coherent doctrine |
| Equipment quality | The capability of what you actually fielded | Modernize selectively, not broadly |
| Deployment | Where the unit stands when the war begins | Match unit type to sector terrain and threat |

Notice that equipment quality sits third, not first. A well-trained, correctly deployed formation with adequate equipment usually beats a poorly placed formation with excellent equipment. That is the single most important lesson of the late game.

## Common Mistakes in War Footing Episode 4

Because the game launched on October 15, 2026 and currently has only a small number of user reviews, community consensus is still forming. Treat the list below as general wargame discipline applied to this title rather than settled player experience.

- **Buying breadth instead of depth.** Ten half-equipped formations lose to four complete ones.
- **Skipping air defense.** It is the easiest category to postpone and the most expensive to lack.
- **Treating politics as optional.** A stable government funds a longer war than an unstable one.
- **Shopping in the final weeks.** Late money belongs in training and sustainment, not new contracts.
- **Deploying by habit.** Sector assignment should follow the enemy configuration, not your unit list order.

## Scenario Setup: A Pre-Campaign Checklist

War Footing includes eight scenarios, each with distinct starting conditions, resource constraints, and enemy configurations. Before Turn 1, run through this checklist so your plan matches the scenario instead of the last one you played.

| Variable | What to check before Turn 1 | Why it changes your plan |
|---|---|---|
| Starting conditions | The force you already have and its doctrine | Tells you what to add rather than rebuild |
| Resource constraints | Budget size and how reliable it is | Sets your realistic procurement ceiling |
| Enemy configuration | Threat shape and likely sectors | Determines what to buy and where to station it |
| Political situation | Stability level and diplomatic obligations | Predicts whether the budget survives the campaign |

## FAQ

### Is War Footing Episode 4 a DLC or a separate release?

Neither. War Footing Episode 4 is the fourth installment of this tutorial series, not a downloadable add-on. The game itself shipped as a single release on October 15, 2026, with eight scenarios included from day one.

### How long does a full campaign take?

Because each turn represents one week and scenario lengths vary, campaign duration depends heavily on which of the eight scenarios you choose and how much time you spend reviewing each turn. Plan for a slow, deliberate pace — this is a game about decisions, not reflexes.

### Do I need to read the earlier episodes first?

Not strictly. The earlier parts of the series cover budgeting and basic procurement, so they help, but this episode stands on its own if you already understand the turn structure. If you are brand new, start with the demo and work forward.

### Should I try the demo before buying?

Yes. A free demo is available on Steam, and it is the fastest way to confirm that the minimalist, text-heavy presentation suits you. War Footing Episode 4 strategies assume you are comfortable reading numbers rather than watching battles play out in real time.
