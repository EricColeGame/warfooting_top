A single overlapping installment payment can end a campaign faster than any enemy offensive. That is the whole reason this war footing procurement guide treats buying as the last step of a plan rather than the first — you decide which formations you need, what they cost to keep alive, and only then sign the order. Get that sequence right and your opening turns produce a force that can actually fight. Get it wrong and you field a paper army your economy can no longer carry.

The good news is that procurement in War Footing rewards discipline far more than it rewards raw spending. Players who buy slowly, train completely, and protect their reserves consistently outperform players who empty the treasury on turn one and spend the next ten turns reacting to bills they forgot about.

## The Turn Cycle: Where Procurement Actually Fits

Every turn moves through the same six phases, and procurement sits in the middle for a reason. It is downstream of budgeting and upstream of training. Skip a phase and the whole loop frays.

| Phase | What You Do | What to Verify First |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Review | Assess the national situation | Funds, income, stability, readiness, threats |
| Budget | Set a defense budget you can sustain | Recurring costs versus available income |
| Procure | Order equipment for planned formations | Availability and staged payment dates |
| Train | Raise readiness of priority units | Personnel and equipment status |
| Deploy | Position formations against likely threats | Approaches, reserves, unit readiness |
| Stabilize | Protect domestic politics | Stability after any major budget shift |

The practical rule experienced players repeat: check your available funds and due payments *before* committing to anything new. Most early collapses are not caused by an enemy breakthrough — they are caused by obligations accepted two turns ago that quietly came due.

## Budget Before You Buy: Income, Capital, and the Installment Trap

Economic planning in this game is about timing as much as total wealth. Recurring income, capital funding, industrial investment, domestic stability, diplomacy, and military procurement all draw from the same pool. Your job is to separate near-term commitments from capacity investments, and to always keep enough liquidity for installment payments.

| Budget Item | Its Purpose | Common Beginner Mistake |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recurring income | Sustains ongoing operating costs | Funding permanent costs with one-time capital |
| Capital funding | Major purchases and industrial expansion | Burning it on everyday operations |
| Installment orders | Large arms buys paid over several turns | Overlapping payment dates with no reserve |
| Emergency reserve | Absorbs sudden military or political costs | Spending the treasury down to zero |
| Stability spending | Keeps domestic politics functional | Cutting it for a short-term military surge |

### Operating Income

- Covers every-turn costs such as readiness, training, and stability.
- Must stay above your recurring obligations at all times.
- Should be re-checked after every major commitment, not just at the start of a turn.

### Capital Funding

- Reserve it for high-impact purchases, industrial expansion, and major equipment.
- Never treat a one-time boost as if it were permanent revenue.
- If a purchase requires capital, ask what you are *not* building instead.

### Emergency Reserve

- Protects you against delayed income and sudden crises.
- Should be grown deliberately before a likely escalation.
- Is for critical gaps, not broad force expansion.

The installment trap deserves its own warning. Before accepting a large arms order, confirm that the future payments will not collide with training, stability, or emergency defense spending. Several overlapping installment dates can wreck an otherwise sound budget — and this is the single most common failure reported in community discussions of the early game.

## The War Footing Procurement Guide, Step by Step

A war footing procurement guide is only useful if it tells you what to do in what order. Procurement should follow an organizational plan: decide which companies, battalions, and brigades you need first, then buy the equipment that makes those formations functional rather than a pile of disconnected hardware.

1. **Plan your first formations.** Identify the equipment your first deploying formations require, and compare immediate availability against long-term production capacity before you order anything.
2. **Purchase deliberately.** Account for the sticker cost plus any staged payments. Never let the force grow faster than you can equip and train it.
3. **Train priority formations first.** Training is what converts equipment and personnel into combat value. A formation can exist on paper and still be useless in a hard fight.
4. **Deploy with the defense plan in mind.** Put defensive formations near likely approaches, hold mobile forces back as reinforcement, and match deployment to each unit's actual readiness.
5. **Recheck stability and budget.** Watch political stability after major spending changes, and enter the war holding reserves instead of an exhausted treasury.

Build from the company and battalion level upward, then combine those elements into brigades with a coherent mission. A smaller, complete, trained force consistently outperforms a large collection of half-equipped formations.

| Procurement Priority | Equipment Category | Buy It When | Hold Off When |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Infantry kit | You are forming your first defensive battalions | Almost never — this is your structural backbone |
| 2 | Air defense | You have key assets worth protecting | You would have to spread systems thin across the map |
| 3 | Armored vehicles | You can concentrate them into one mobile formation | You are tempted to sprinkle them across every brigade |
| 4 | Helicopters | Crew proficiency can be funded | You cannot afford the training that makes them work |
| 5 | Special forces | You have a specific, selective mission | You plan to use them as mass infantry |

## Unit Types and Training Priorities

Not every unit deserves the same investment, and not every unit tolerates being rushed into combat. The table below reflects what the community generally treats as the sensible training order.

| Unit Type | Primary Role | Training Priority |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Infantry | Hold ground; core of defensive battalions | High before deployment |
| Armored | Protected mobility and counterattack | Very high — coordination is complex |
| Air Defense | Protect key formations and infrastructure | High — position decides its value |
| Helicopters | Rapid response and mobility | Very high — crew proficiency is critical |
| Special Forces | Selective specialized missions | Extreme — never deploy them untrained |

A few takeaways by category:

- **Infantry:** broad coverage beats a handful of over-equipped units. Infantry is the structure every larger formation is built on.
- **Armor:** concentrate vehicles into formations with a clear mobile role instead of scattering them.
- **Air defense:** limited systems belong around your most important assets, not spread across the map.
- **Special forces:** readiness and quality matter more than numbers. Massing them discards their main advantage.

Availability frequently beats quality in the early game. A unit you can field and train now usually contributes more than a superior unit that arrives after the fighting starts.

## Deployment, Stability, and the Pre-Invasion Checklist

Use diplomacy when extra preparation time is worth more than another purchase, and keep enough domestic investment flowing to prevent avoidable disruption during mobilization. A strong-looking army that the state can no longer afford is a fragile one — rising budget strain is a strategic signal, not just a spreadsheet problem.

| Pre-Invasion Check | Why It Matters |
| --- | --- |
| Recurring income covers all obligations | Prevents mid-campaign funding collapse |
| Emergency reserve protected and growing | Absorbs delayed income and surprises |
| First-line formations fully equipped and trained | Removes paper-strength failures |
| Defensive deployment covers likely approaches | Denies the enemy easy openings |
| Mobile reserve identified and positioned | Lets you respond instead of reacting |
| Stability stable after budget changes | Keeps the war economy functional |
| Installment payments scheduled without collisions | Avoids the classic overlapping-bill spiral |

If you can tick every row above, you are ready to escalate. If you cannot, another turn of preparation is usually cheaper than a broken front.

## FAQ

**Q: Should I spend my entire treasury in the first turns?**

No. Sustainable spending beats a turn-one spending spree every time. Keep recurring obligations affordable, protect an emergency reserve, and let your force grow at a rate your income can actually support. This is the central lesson of any war footing procurement guide.

**Q: What matters more, equipment quality or numbers?**

In the opening turns, availability and completeness usually win. A full, trained formation of modest equipment outperforms several incomplete formations of better gear. Quality becomes decisive once your economy and training pipeline can support it.

**Q: Why did my full-strength unit perform poorly in combat?**

Paper strength is not readiness. Units need completed training, suitable equipment, and correct deployment. Deploying under-trained or under-equipped formations is one of the most frequent beginner mistakes, according to player experience shared in community threads.

**Q: How much should I invest in diplomacy as a new player?**

Invest only when the extra preparation time is worth more than another purchase. Diplomacy is a tool for buying turns, not a permanent budget line you should fund by starving training or stability spending.
