War Footing rewards players who plan three turns ahead and punishes anyone who treats the treasury like a one-time shopping spree. A good war footing game walkthrough should teach the rhythm of the turn cycle — review, budget, procure, train, deploy, stabilize — rather than hand you a list of units to buy. Whether you are three turns into a first campaign or restarting after an economic collapse, the same fundamentals decide whether your mobilization holds together. This war footing game walkthrough covers the decisions that matter most in your opening turns and the habits that keep a war economy alive long after them.

## The Six-Phase Turn Cycle Every War Footing Game Walkthrough Starts With

The turn cycle is the spine of the game, and any war footing game walkthrough that skips it leaves you guessing at everything else. Each turn runs through the same six phases, and the order matters: you cannot sensibly deploy what you never budgeted for, and you cannot train formations you never equipped.

| Phase | What You Do | What to Verify Before Moving On |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Review | Assess the national picture | Funds, income, stability, readiness, threats |
| Budget | Set a defense budget you can sustain | Recurring costs vs. available income |
| Procure | Order equipment for planned formations | Availability and staged payment dates |
| Train | Raise readiness on priority units | Personnel and equipment status |
| Deploy | Position formations against likely threats | Approaches covered, mobile reserve ready |
| Stabilize | Protect domestic politics | Stability after major budget shifts |

The most common opening mistake, based on player experience shared across community forums and wikis, is treating turn one as a shopping trip. You start with a fat treasury, so you buy the biggest formations you can afford — and then discover two turns later that installment payments, readiness costs, and stability spending all come due at once.

### Begin Every Turn With the Money Check

Before committing to anything, look at the funds you have and the payments you already owe. Obligations you accepted earlier keep coming due whether or not you still want them. That quick ledger check takes seconds and prevents the cascade of unpaid costs that ends most early campaigns. An army that cannot pay its own readiness bill stops being an army very quickly.

## Budgeting for a Long War: Three Pools You Cannot Mix

Economic planning in War Footing is about timing as much as total wealth. Recurring income, capital funding, industrial investment, domestic stability, diplomacy, and procurement all draw on the same pot of money. Separating near-term commitments from long-term capacity investments is what keeps a mobilization sustainable instead of a two-turn sprint.

| Budget Item | What It Pays For | Beginner Mistake |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recurring income | Every-turn costs: readiness, training, stability | Funding permanent costs with one-time capital |
| Capital funding | Major purchases and industrial expansion | Spending it on routine operations |
| Installment orders | Large arms deals paid over time | Letting payment dates overlap with no reserve |
| Emergency reserve | Sudden military or political costs | Draining the treasury to zero |
| Stability spending | Domestic politics during mobilization | Cutting it to fund a short-term military surge |

Sustainable spending beats dumping your treasury in turn one. Recurring income has to stay above recurring obligations, because readiness, training, and stability are billed every single turn. Capital funding belongs to high-impact purchases and industrial expansion, not to covering everyday operations. And the emergency reserve exists to absorb delayed income or a sudden crisis, which means it should be growing *before* tensions rise, not after the shooting starts.

### The Installment Trap

Large arms orders are typically paid in stages rather than all at once. Two or three overlapping payment dates can collapse an otherwise sound budget, so map the schedule before you sign. If a purchase would collide with training or stability costs, delay it a turn rather than gambling on income that may not arrive.

## Procurement and Training: Build Formations, Not Shopping Lists

Procurement should follow an organizational plan. Decide which companies, battalions, and brigades you actually need first, then buy the equipment that makes those formations functional. Disconnected purchases — a few tanks here, a helicopter there — look impressive on a ledger and accomplish almost nothing on the map.

This is the part of the war footing game walkthrough where patience pays off most. Work through these steps in order:

1. **Plan your first formations.** Identify exactly what equipment your opening formations require. Compare what is available right now against what production can deliver later.
2. **Purchase deliberately.** Count the full cost, including staged payments, and never grow the force faster than you can equip and train it.
3. **Train priority formations first.** Training determines whether equipment and personnel actually perform. A formation can exist on paper while being nowhere near ready for hard operations.
4. **Deploy with the defense plan in mind.** Place defensive formations near likely approaches, keep mobile forces back as reinforcement, and match deployment to real readiness levels.
5. **Recheck stability and budget.** Watch domestic politics after major spending shifts, and enter the fight with reserves rather than an exhausted treasury.

| Formation Level | Purpose | What to Confirm Before Building Up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Company | Smallest functional combat element | Full equipment and a clear role |
| Battalion | Core defensive or maneuver unit | Coherent mission, training underway |
| Brigade | Combines battalions for a specific job | Supporting arms, logistics, readiness |

Build from the company and battalion level upward, then combine those elements into brigades with a coherent mission. A smaller, complete, trained force reliably outperforms a larger collection of half-equipped formations — and it is far cheaper to keep in the field.

## Unit Types at a Glance: Roles Before Raw Quality

Availability often matters more than raw quality in the opening turns. A unit you can field and train now beats a superior system that arrives after the fighting has already started. That said, role still determines how you spend your limited training budget.

| Unit Type | Primary Role | Training Priority | Common Beginner Error |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Infantry | Hold ground; core of defensive battalions | High before deployment | Stretching too few units across too much frontage |
| Armored | Protected mobility and counterattack | Very high — coordination heavy | Scattering vehicles across every brigade |
| Air Defense | Shield key formations and infrastructure | High — position decides value | Spreading systems thin across the map |
| Helicopters | Rapid response and mobility | Very high — crew proficiency critical | Treating them as static fire support |
| Special Forces | Selective, high-value missions | Extreme — never deploy untrained | Throwing them into mass formations |

A few role-specific habits worth building early:

- **Infantry:** broad coverage beats a handful of over-equipped units. Infantry is the structure every larger formation is built around.
- **Armor:** concentrate vehicles into formations with a clear mobile mission instead of sprinkling them everywhere.
- **Air defense:** limited systems belong near your most valuable assets, not scattered to cover everything.
- **Special forces:** quality and readiness matter more than numbers. Using them as line infantry throws away their main advantage.

## Politics, Diplomacy, and the Pre-Invasion Checklist

Diplomacy is worth the investment when extra preparation time is worth more than another purchase. Buying a turn of breathing room can be cheaper than buying a brigade, and it keeps your domestic situation stable while you mobilize. Stability spending matters for the same reason: rising budget strain is a strategic signal, not a spreadsheet annoyance. A strong-looking army that the state can no longer afford is a fragile one.

| Pre-Invasion Check | Why It Matters | Red Flag |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recurring income covers all obligations | Prevents mid-campaign insolvency | Income already fully committed |
| Emergency reserve protected and growing | Absorbs delayed income and crises | Treasury sitting at zero |
| First-line formations equipped and trained | Paper strength fails in combat | Units stuck at partial readiness |
| Defensive deployment covers likely approaches | Blunts and deters the first push | Gaps on the obvious axes |
| Mobile reserve identified and positioned | Answers breakthroughs | No uncommitted force available |
| Stability stable after budget changes | Keeps mobilization functional | Unrest spiking after spending |
| Installment payments scheduled without collisions | Protects cash flow | Overlapping due dates |

If your war footing game walkthrough playthrough keeps stalling around the same turn, the cause is usually economic rather than tactical. Fix the budget and the battlefield tends to follow.

## Beginner FAQ

**Should I spend my entire treasury in the first turns?**

No. Spending everything feels powerful for one turn and then locks you out of training, stability, and emergency spending. Keep a reserve, confirm your installment dates do not collide, and expand at the pace your income can support.

**What matters more, equipment quality or numbers?**

For most new players, availability and readiness matter more than raw quality. A complete, trained formation beats a larger half-equipped one, and it stays affordable turn after turn. Concentrate your best equipment where it has a defined role.

**Why did my full-strength unit perform poorly in combat?**

Paper strength is not readiness. Units need completed training, suitable equipment, and correct deployment. Sending under-trained or under-equipped formations into a real fight is one of the most common beginner mistakes in any war footing game walkthrough.

**How much should I invest in diplomacy as a new player?**

Enough to buy preparation time when that time is worth more than another purchase — and not so much that stability spending suffers.
