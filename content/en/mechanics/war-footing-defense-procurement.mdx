## What "War Footing" Actually Means for Defense Procurement

Speed is now the currency of military advantage, and the machinery that buys, builds, and delivers weapons is being rebuilt around that single idea. In November 2025, the U.S. Department of War issued a memorandum that placed its entire acquisition enterprise on a **war footing defense procurement** model — treating the purchase of weapons as a warfighting function rather than a paperwork exercise. That shift matters far beyond the Pentagon: it changes who holds authority over programs, how money moves, which companies can compete, and how quickly a requirement becomes a fielded capability.

The logic behind war footing defense procurement is simple to state and brutally hard to execute. Peacetime acquisition optimizes for compliance, exhaustive requirements, and avoiding mistakes. Wartime acquisition optimizes for delivery — getting relevant capability into the hands of warfighters before the threat changes shape. When an organization flips from the first mindset to the second, nearly every rule, incentive, and reporting line has to change with it.

The most visible signal was structural: the Defense Acquisition System was redesignated as a "Warfighting Acquisition System," and the Secretary directed that speed to capability delivery become the organizing principle for the entire enterprise. You can read the full directive in [the acquisition transformation memorandum published by the Department of War](https://media.defense.gov/2025/nov/10/2003819439/-1/-1/1/transforming-the-defense-acquisition-system-into-the-warfighting-acquisition-system-to-accelerate-fielding-of-urgently-needed-capabilities-to-our-warriors.pdf).

| Dimension | Peacetime posture | War footing posture |
|---|---|---|
| Organizing principle | Process fidelity and compliance | Speed to capability delivery |
| Success metric | Every requirement satisfied | Capability in the warfighter's hands |
| Risk tolerance | Avoid failure at all costs | Accept calculated, shared risk |
| Program structure | Large monolithic programs | Schedule-driven capability increments |
| Contracting | Standard regulation-heavy instruments | Commercial-first, flexible agreements |
| Industrial base | Steady-state, low-volume output | Surge-ready, multi-source capacity |
| Oversight | Broad, layered reviews | By-exception, data-driven scorecards |

## The Three Systemic Failures That Forced the Shift

The directive is unusually blunt about why the old system struggled. It names three root causes for slow delivery times, and each one points to a different repair.

**Fragmented accountability.** No single leader owned a program's outcome end to end. Authority was scattered across requirements writers, program offices, test organizations, and budget staff, so nobody could make a fast trade without convening a committee.

**Broken incentives.** The system rewarded programs for satisfying every specification, even when doing so pushed delivery years to the right. On-time delivery was a nice-to-have; requirement purity was the scorecard.

**Procurement behaviors that deter investment.** Unpredictable demand, heavy documentation, and cost pass-throughs discouraged companies from building capacity — leaving an industrial base that could not surge when called upon.

| Failure | What it looks like on the ground | Corrective direction |
|---|---|---|
| Fragmented accountability | No single owner for schedule or outcome | Portfolio Acquisition Executives with real authority |
| Broken incentives | 100% requirement satisfaction beats on-time delivery | Requirement trades prioritized by time-to-field; pay tied to delivery |
| Investment-deterring behavior | Unstable quantities, pass-through fees | Stable multi-year demand signals, direct-to-supplier deals |

## The Mechanics: What Actually Changes Inside the System

This is where the transformation gets concrete. A war footing defense procurement model is not a slogan — it is a set of mechanical changes to how requirements are written, how budgets move, and how systems are tested.

| Mechanic | Legacy approach | War footing approach |
|---|---|---|
| Requirements | System-specific technical documents | Portfolio-level outcomes; capability trade councils |
| Budget | Rigid line items, slow reprogramming | Portfolio flexibility, delegated below-threshold realignment |
| Contracting | Regulation-heavy, documentation-first | Commercial-first, non-standard instruments where practicable |
| Testing | Full requalification for any change | Accredited pipelines, reciprocity, defined substitution boundaries |
| Architecture | Closed, vendor-locked designs | Modular Open System Architecture with government purpose rights |
| Sourcing | Single prime, long-term sole source | Two-to-production standard: at least two qualified sources |

Two of these deserve extra attention. First, the shift from requirements-based to solutions-based buying means industry can propose approaches that meet the operational problem without matching every line of a government specification. Second, the "two-to-production" standard pushes programs to keep at least two qualified suppliers for critical content through initial production — a direct hedge against the single-point-of-failure risk that has plagued several high-profile programs.

Testing reform is the quiet enabler. If a component swap triggers a full system requalification, nobody will ever swap components quickly. Accredited test pipelines and reciprocity between test organizations are what make modularity real instead of theoretical. The Adaptive Acquisition Framework maintained on [Acquisition.gov](https://www.acquisition.gov/) remains the governing structure these pathways live inside.

## Portfolio Acquisition Executives: One Throat to Choke

The centerpiece of the reorganization is the Portfolio Acquisition Executive, or PAE. Instead of dozens of program offices reporting up separate chains, existing Program Executive Offices are reorganized into portfolios of like capabilities, each with a single accountable leader.

The authority chain is deliberately short: it runs from the Defense Acquisition Executive to the Service Acquisition Executives to the PAEs. Contracting officers are aligned to report directly into that chain, so the person accountable for delivering a capability also controls the levers needed to deliver it.

| Role | Core responsibility | Key authority |
|---|---|---|
| Defense Acquisition Executive | Enterprise-level direction | Cross-department program decisions |
| Service Acquisition Executive | Service-level portfolio oversight | Statutory authority over service programs |
| Portfolio Acquisition Executive | Single owner of portfolio outcomes | Structure increments, trade requirements, shift funds, align contracting |
| Program Manager | Day-to-day execution | Make trades within thresholds, escalate above them |

Personnel policy changes alongside the structure. The directive calls for longer, delivery-matched tenures — a four-year minimum with two-year extensions — with incentive compensation tied to capability delivery time and mission outcomes, plus removal mechanisms for chronic schedule setbacks. In plain terms: give leaders enough time to matter, pay them for results, and hold them accountable when schedules slip.

## Rebuilding the Industrial Base So It Can Surge

You cannot buy at wartime speed from a peacetime industrial base. The transformation strategy is candid that the defense industrial base has narrowed dramatically — from dozens of prime vendors after the Cold War to a handful of major primes developing the most critical weapon systems today. That concentration delivers exquisite systems at low volume, but it is brittle.

The fix runs on several tracks:

- **Stabilize demand signals.** Multi-year procurements, long-duration contracts, and fenced funding give companies the confidence to invest in factories and tooling.
- **Go direct-to-supplier.** Negotiating with lower-tier suppliers directly avoids pass-through costs and exposes fragile nodes such as rocket motors, sensors, and fuzes.
- **Expand competition.** Lower barriers to entry, fund prototyping, and deliberately include non-traditional vendors in source selection.
- **Pull in private capital.** Regular engagement with venture and private equity firms helps channel investment toward production capacity rather than just R&D.

A new Wartime Production Unit is intended to knit these threads together, pairing government contracting specialists with manufacturing and supply chain experts to accelerate production of critical weapons and munitions.

| Lever | Problem addressed | Intended outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Stable multi-year demand | Year-to-year quantity swings | Willingness to invest in capacity |
| Direct-to-supplier deals | Pass-through costs, hidden single points of failure | Lower cost, better supply chain visibility |
| Two-to-production standard | Sole-source fragility | Competitive, resilient sourcing |
| Modular open architectures | Vendor lock-in | Faster technology insertion, more bidders |
| Private capital engagement | Chronic underinvestment | Faster factory expansion, new entrants |

## Actionable Takeaways for Contractors and Program Teams

If you work in or around defense procurement, the practical implications of a war footing defense procurement posture are already visible in solicitations and evaluation criteria.

1. **Lead with schedule.** Proposals that demonstrate realistic, aggressive delivery timelines — not just technical elegance — align with how portfolios are now scored.
2. **Bring alternatives.** If your product solves the operational problem through a different technical approach, say so explicitly and back it with demonstration data.
3. **Design for openness.** Systems built on open standards and documented interfaces are more attractive because they reduce requalification risk later.
4. **Think about the second source.** Prime contractors should expect pressure to qualify alternate suppliers; subcontractors should position themselves as the credible second source.
5. **Track portfolio scorecards.** Programs are increasingly assessed on time from validated need to initial and full operational capability, not on activity volume.

| Stakeholder | Immediate action | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Prime contractor | Qualify a second source for critical content | Reduces program risk, strengthens bid position |
| Non-traditional vendor | Demonstrate a working prototype early | Solutions-based buying rewards demonstrated capability |
| Program office | Map trades against time-to-field | Aligns decisions with portfolio scorecards |
| Contracting officer | Learn flexible agreement instruments | Enables faster award and follow-on production |

## FAQ

**What does it mean to put defense procurement on a war footing?**
It means reorganizing the entire acquisition enterprise around speed to capability delivery. Authority concentrates in accountable portfolio leaders, requirements become tradable, contracting gets more flexible, and the industrial base is deliberately built to surge.

**Does a war footing defense procurement model mean less oversight?**
Not less oversight — different oversight. Reviews move toward a by-exception model driven by real-time program data and portfolio scorecards, so leadership attention lands on programs showing cost, schedule, or performance problems rather than on every program equally.

**How does this affect small and non-traditional defense contractors?**
Generally favorably. The strategy explicitly calls for lowering barriers to entry, using prototyping and consortium marketplaces, and including non-traditional vendors in source selection. The catch is that you still need demonstrable capability, not just a promising concept.

**What is the biggest risk of moving to a war footing?**
Trading requirements for speed can go wrong if the trades are not disciplined. The directive acknowledges this by pairing speed with rigor — technology and manufacturing readiness assessments, cost realism, and test pipelines that keep safety and suitability intact while compressing timelines.

The bottom line: war footing defense procurement is less about buying more and more about buying faster, with clearer accountability and an industrial base that can actually scale. Programs that internalize that shift will find the new system rewards them; programs that wait for the old rhythm to return will keep missing schedules.
