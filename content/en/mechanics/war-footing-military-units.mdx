## Why War Footing Military Units Reward Planning Over Panic Buying

In War Footing, war footing military units are not shopping-list items. They are complete formations — equipment, personnel, and readiness — and all three have to line up before a single one of them can fight. Dump your treasury on turn one and you will field an impressive-looking order of battle with almost no real combat power behind it.

That is why experienced players treat war footing military units as a logistics problem first and a combat problem second. You are not buying tanks; you are buying the ability to sustain, train, and supply a formation that will still be functional ten turns from now. The players who win the opening phase are rarely the ones who spend the most — they are the ones who spend in an order that keeps every formation complete.

This guide walks through the core turn cycle, the budget rules that keep mobilization affordable, and the unit-by-unit training priorities that separate a real army from a parade.

## The Six-Phase Turn Cycle Every Commander Should Follow

Every turn in War Footing runs through the same sequence. Skipping a phase is how players end up surprised by a payment they forgot or a unit that was never trained.

| Phase | What You Do | What to Verify |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Review | Assess the national situation | Funds, income, stability, readiness, active threats |
| Budget | Set a defense budget you can sustain | Recurring costs vs. available income |
| Procure | Order equipment for planned formations | Availability and staged payment dates |
| Train | Raise readiness on priority units | Personnel and equipment status |
| Deploy | Position formations against likely approaches | Reserves and reinforcement routes |
| Stabilize | Protect domestic politics | Stability after major budget shifts |

**Rule of thumb:** open every turn by checking available funds and due payments *before* committing to anything new. Community reports from beginner walkthroughs consistently point to the same failure pattern — players collapse not because they bought too little, but because they forgot an obligation they accepted two turns earlier.

Treat the cycle as a checklist rather than a menu. You can move through it quickly once it becomes habit, but you should never reorder it.

## Budgeting: Where War Footing Military Units Actually Get Paid For

Economic planning in War Footing is about timing as much as total wealth. Recurring income, capital funding, industrial investment, domestic stability, diplomacy, and procurement all pull from the same pool. Separate near-term commitments from long-term capacity investments, and always keep liquidity for installment payments.

| Budget Item | Purpose | Common Beginner Mistake |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recurring income | Sustains every-turn operating costs | Funding permanent costs with one-time capital |
| Capital funding | Major purchases and industrial expansion | Spending it on routine operations |
| Installment orders | Large arms purchases paid over time | Overlapping payment dates with no reserve |
| Emergency reserve | Absorbs sudden military or political costs | Spending the treasury down to zero |
| Stability spending | Keeps domestic politics functional | Cutting it for a short-term military surge |

### Operating Income

- Covers every-turn costs: readiness, training, and stability
- Must stay above your recurring obligations at all times
- Recheck it after every major commitment

### Capital Funding

- Reserve it for high-impact purchases
- Best used on industrial expansion and major equipment
- Never treat a one-time boost as permanent revenue

### Emergency Reserve

- Protects against delayed income and sudden crises
- Grow it *before* a likely escalation, not during one
- Spend it on critical gaps, not broad expansion

**The installment trap.** Before accepting a large arms order, confirm that future payments will not collide with training, stability, or emergency defense spending. Two or three overlapping installment dates can wreck an otherwise healthy budget, and the resulting stability hit often costs more than the equipment was worth.

## Build Formations From the Company Up

Procurement should follow an organizational plan. Decide which companies, battalions, and brigades you need first, then buy the equipment that makes those formations functional — not a pile of disconnected hardware.

| Step | Action | Why It Matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Plan your first formations | Identify the equipment each one requires before ordering |
| 2 | Purchase deliberately | Account for purchase cost *plus* staged payments |
| 3 | Train priority formations first | Training determines whether equipment performs in combat |
| 4 | Deploy against the defense plan | Match placement to each unit's actual readiness |
| 5 | Recheck stability and budget | Enter the fight with reserves, not an empty treasury |

**The formation rule:** build from the company and battalion level upward, then combine those elements into brigades with a coherent mission. A smaller, complete, trained force consistently outperforms several half-equipped formations — a point that shows up repeatedly in player experience threads about early-game collapses.

## Unit Roles and Training Priorities

Not every war footing military unit needs the same investment. Each type has a role, and each role has a different tolerance for undertrained crews.

| Unit Type | Primary Role | Training Priority |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Infantry | Hold ground; core of defensive battalions | High — before any deployment |
| Armored | Protected mobility and counterattack | Very high — coordination is complex |
| Air Defense | Protect key formations and infrastructure | High — position decides value |
| Helicopters | Rapid response and mobility | Very high — crew proficiency is critical |
| Special Forces | Selective, high-value missions | Extreme — never deploy untrained |

Key takeaways by category:

- **Infantry:** broad coverage beats a few over-equipped units. Infantry is the structure every larger formation is built on.
- **Armor:** concentrate vehicles into formations with a clear mobile role instead of sprinkling them across every brigade.
- **Air defense:** limited systems belong around your most important assets, not spread thin across the map.
- **Special forces:** quality and readiness matter more than numbers. Massing them into ordinary formations throws away their only real advantage.

If you have to choose between another unit and finishing the training on the units you already own, finish the training. Availability beats theoretical quality when the shooting starts.

## Readiness, Deployment, and Political Stability

Deployment is where preparation either pays off or falls apart. Place defensive formations near likely approaches, keep mobile forces back as reinforcement, and coordinate every position with the unit's real readiness — not its on-paper strength.

| Unit Status | What It Looks Like | Expected Combat Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trained and fully equipped | Complete formation, ready crews | Reliable performance |
| Equipped but untrained | Full roster, low readiness | Underperforms badly |
| Trained but under-equipped | Skilled crews, missing kit | Fragile, easily attrited |
| Neither | Paper strength only | Collapses on contact |

### Pre-Invasion Checklist

| Check | Target State |
| --- | --- |
| Recurring income | Covers all ongoing obligations |
| Emergency reserve | Protected and growing |
| First-line formations | Fully equipped and trained |
| Defensive deployment | Covers likely approaches |
| Mobile reserve | Identified and positioned |
| Political stability | Stable after budget changes |
| Installment payments | Scheduled with no collisions |

Diplomacy is worth the investment when extra preparation time is more valuable than another purchase. ## FAQ

**How many war footing military units should I build in the first ten turns?**

Fewer than you think. Aim for one or two complete, trained formations rather than five incomplete ones. Community reports from beginner walkthroughs are consistent on this: sustainable spending beats dumping the treasury in turn one, because recurring obligations are what actually break a run.

**What matters more — equipment quality or unit numbers?**

Quality, up to a point. Numbers without readiness produce paper strength that fails in combat. Build a complete core first, then expand once your income comfortably covers the added every-turn costs.

**Why did my full-strength unit perform poorly in combat?**

Paper strength is not readiness. Units need completed training, suitable equipment, and correct deployment. Deploying under-trained or under-equipped formations is one of the most common beginner mistakes in War Footing, and the fix is almost always patience rather than more purchases.

**How much should I invest in diplomacy as a new player?**

Enough to buy time when time is worth more than equipment. If a diplomatic delay lets you finish training your first-line formations and grow your reserve, it is usually the better buy. If it just postpones a fight you are already prepared for, spend the money on readiness instead.
