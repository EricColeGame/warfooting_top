The first shot hasn't been fired yet — and the war may already be decided. In War Footing, you never command a single soldier in the field; you command the budget, the factories, the contracts, and the politics that put those soldiers there. That makes **war footing resource management** the single most important skill in the game, and the one most new ministers of defense get wrong. Get **war footing resource management** right, and the invasion runs into a prepared, well-equipped, combined-arms force. Get it wrong, and you fight with whatever you happened to buy first.

This guide breaks down how the resource systems actually interact, which trade-offs matter most, and how to structure your weekly turns so the army that meets the enemy is the army you meant to build.

## What War Footing Resource Management Really Means

Most strategy games hand you a battlefield. War Footing hands you a calendar and a country that may not survive the year. You play as the Minister of Defense, and every turn represents a single week of preparation before an invasion you can see coming but cannot stop. There is no tactical micro during the fighting — battles resolve at the operational level, sector by sector, week by week, based entirely on what you assembled beforehand.

That design choice is why resource management sits at the center of the experience. The phrase itself comes from real-world defense economics: a nation shifting its industry, budget, and manpower toward military readiness. Here, you live that shift one week at a time.

The loop is deceptively simple. Each week you source equipment from foreign suppliers or your own domestic industry, train and assign units, and manage political stability so the money keeps flowing. Then the invasion begins. As the developers put it, the army you built is the army you fight with — and nothing from the preparation phase can be undone once the shooting starts.

## The Four Resource Pools You're Actually Balancing

New players assume the budget is the only resource. It isn't. Four pools interact constantly, and draining one to fill another is the most common way a scenario collapses.

| Resource pool | Where it comes from | Where it drains | Early warning sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| Funding | Political stability that keeps appropriations flowing | Procurement contracts, training, unit upkeep | You start deferring purchases week after week |
| Time | A fixed weekly turn — nothing buys more of it | Every decision consumes it | The invasion arrives before your brigades are ready |
| Political stability | Careful management of domestic politics | Neglect, unpopular trade-offs, overreach | Budget flow slows exactly when you need hardware |
| Access | Domestic production lines and foreign suppliers | Dependency on one source, strained obligations | The equipment you want isn't available at any price |

The access pool is the one people underestimate. An equipment type can be affordable and capable and still useless to you because it isn't obtainable within your timeframe. Availability is a real constraint, not a rounding error.

A useful habit: at the start of every week, ask which of the four pools is closest to empty. That pool sets your priority, even if it isn't the most exciting thing on the screen.

## A Phase-by-Phase Framework for Weekly Turns

Because each turn equals one week, it helps to think in phases rather than individual purchases. The table below is a general planning framework, not a rigid script — the eight scenarios ship with different starting conditions, resource constraints, and enemy configurations, so your mileage will vary.

| Phase | Primary goal | Spend priority | What to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening | Protect the budget pipeline | Political stability and cheap, high-availability equipment | Chasing flagship hardware you can't sustain or replace |
| Build-out | Fill structural gaps in the force | Artillery, air defense, and the support types your doctrine assumes | Pouring everything into one equipment family |
| Consolidation | Convert purchases into readiness | Training, unit assignment, and doctrine fit | Buying new types you have no weeks left to integrate |
| Invasion | Fight with what exists | Deployment choices and sector coverage | Panic procurement that arrives too late to matter |

Two rules of thumb carry across all four phases. First, readiness is not automatic — units need training and assignment time, so equipment bought in the final weeks is often equipment wasted. Second, combined arms is rewarded. A force built around a single dominant type tends to fold when the enemy brings the counter to it.

### Why "just buy more" fails

The temptation in any resource game is to convert money into stuff as fast as possible. War Footing punishes that because cost is only one of three axes. Capability and availability matter just as much, and a purchase that ignores doctrine fit produces a unit that looks impressive on a ledger and underperforms in the sector where it's deployed.

## Procurement Priorities: Cost, Capability, and Availability

The game ships with more than 180 equipment types spanning small arms, armor, artillery, aircraft, and air defense systems. Each carries distinct trade-offs across cost, capability, and availability — which means there is no universally correct shopping list, only correct answers for your scenario and doctrine.

| Equipment family | What it contributes | Key question before you commit |
|---|---|---|
| Small arms | Basic infantry lethality and unit readiness | Is the cheap option genuinely sufficient, or will it cap your readiness later? |
| Armor | Breakthrough power and the ability to hold ground | Can your budget and timeline support the sustainment it implies? |
| Artillery | Operational-level fire support across sectors | Does it match the doctrine your force structure assumes? |
| Aircraft | Reach, strike, and battlefield awareness | Do you have air defense protecting the investment behind it? |
| Air defense | Protection that keeps every other purchase alive | Is this a luxury, or the thing that decides whether your army survives week one? |

A practical method: rank your equipment shortlist by availability first, capability second, and cost third. Availability eliminates options permanently, capability shapes outcomes, and cost is usually the constraint you can work around with time. Reversing that order is how players end up with a beautifully equipped force that never arrives.

### Foreign suppliers versus domestic industry

Sourcing abroad and sourcing at home are different bets, not better or worse ones. Foreign suppliers can offer capability your domestic industry can't match yet, but they tie you to diplomatic relationships and external availability. Domestic production trades short-term capability for predictability. Community reports from the demo period consistently point to the same lesson: whichever route you choose, don't depend on a single source for a critical capability.

## Politics, Stability, and the Mistakes That Break a Budget

Military spending doesn't happen in a vacuum. Diplomatic obligations and domestic political stability compete for exactly the same resources your procurement does, and ignoring them has consequences that show up later in the budget line.

| Pressure | What it costs you | How to manage it |
|---|---|---|
| Domestic political stability | Directly throttles the funding you receive | Treat it as a resource with a floor, not a leftover |
| Diplomatic obligations | Consumes budget and political capital | Honor them early, when the price is lowest |
| Supplier dependency | Turns availability into a single point of failure | Diversify, or invest in domestic capacity before you need it |
| Overreach on procurement | Short-term force growth, long-term instability | Pace purchases to what stability can support |

### Five mistakes that sink a defense budget

- **Buying before deciding doctrine.** Equipment quality only pays off when it fits the force you're actually fielding.
- **Treating politics as background noise.** When stability drops, the budget flow drops with it — usually at the worst possible moment.
- **Building a mono-type army.** Combined arms and knowing your enemy's composition are explicitly rewarded in combat resolution.
- **Neglecting readiness.** Training and assignment take weeks. Untrained units with good gear still lose sectors.
- **Spending late.** Procurement in the final turns rarely converts into deployable combat power before the invasion lands.

Player experience during the demo suggests the most reliable improvement for struggling runs isn't a better purchase list — it's a better calendar. Decide in week one what your force must look like in the final week, then work backward.

## FAQ

**What is war footing resource management in this game?**
It's the practice of balancing four interconnected pools — funding, time, political stability, and equipment access — across weekly turns so that your military is ready before the invasion begins. Combat is resolved operationally from what you built, so preparation decisions effectively are the battle.

**How long is a campaign, and does every scenario play the same?**
Each turn represents one week, and the game includes eight scenarios with distinct starting conditions, resource constraints, and enemy configurations. That means the correct spending plan in one scenario can be a losing plan in another.

**Should I buy from foreign suppliers or build domestically?**
It depends on your scenario's timeline and political situation. Foreign suppliers often provide capability you can't produce yet at the cost of dependency; domestic industry is more predictable but slower to mature. Most successful runs mix both rather than committing fully to one.

**Does war footing resource management matter more than combat decisions?**
There are no tactical combat decisions in the traditional sense — battles resolve sector by sector each week based on unit readiness, doctrine fit, equipment quality, and deployment. Your resource choices determine all four of those inputs, which is why preparation is the whole game.

**Is there a way to try before buying?**
Yes. A demo is available, and the game offers 16 Steam achievements plus Steam Cloud and Family Sharing support. System requirements are modest — Windows 10 (64-bit), a dual-core 2.0 GHz processor, 4 GB of RAM, integrated graphics, and about 500 MB of storage — so most machines can run it comfortably.
